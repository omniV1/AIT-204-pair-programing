Generative Adversarial Networks: A Deep Dive into Synthetic Image Generation

1. Problem Statement

In the contemporary digital landscape, synthetic media and "fake images" have transcended the realm of novelty to become significant drivers of public opinion and social discourse. The proliferation of deepfakes poses a challenge to digital authenticity and trust. Consequently, it is imperative for students and researchers to understand the underlying mechanics of image synthesis.

This project aims to bridge this knowledge gap by designing and implementing a foundational Generative Adversarial Network (GAN). By training the network on the MNIST dataset (a collection of 70,000 handwritten digits), we demonstrate how two competing neural networks can interact to learn complex data distributions, transforming pure Gaussian noise into aesthetically coherent and recognizable human handwriting.

2. Algorithm of the Solution

The solution is grounded in Game Theory, specifically a zero-sum game between two actors: the Generator and the Discriminator.

2.1 The Generator (G)

The Generator acts as a "forger." Its goal is to take a random latent vector z from a normal distribution and map it to a high-dimensional image space.

  • Architecture: A 4-layer Fully Connected (Dense) network.
  • Hidden Layers: 256, 512, and 1024 units, each followed by LeakyReLU (alpha=0.2) to mitigate the dying ReLU problem and BatchNorm1d for training stability.
  • Output Layer: 784 units (28x28) with a Tanh activation, ensuring pixel intensities are normalized between -1 and 1.

2.2 The Discriminator (D)

The Discriminator acts as an "expert" or "policeman." Its goal is to distinguish between images from the true distribution (MNIST) and the synthetic distribution (Generator).

  • Architecture: A 3-layer classifier.
  • Hidden Layers: 512, 256, and 128 units, utilizing LeakyReLU and BatchNorm1d on the primary layer.
  • Output Layer: A single scalar with Sigmoid activation, representing the probability that the input image is "Real."

2.3 Adversarial Objective Function

The training process follows the Minimax loss defined by the Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) function:

min_G max_D V(D, G) = E_x[log D(x)] + E_z[log(1 - D(G(z)))]

3. Analysis of Findings & Visualizations

The training was executed for 400 epochs using the Adam Optimizer (lr=0.0002, betas=(0.5, 0.999)). The performance was measured by observing the structural evolution of the generated samples at specific milestones.

3.1 Training Lifecycle Analysis

  • Epoch 1 (Initial Convergence): Unlike pure Gaussian noise, the model immediately focuses on the center of the canvas. Visuals show high-frequency noise artifacts but with a distinct "blob" topology emerging, as the Discriminator aggressively penalizes random edge pixels.
  • Epoch 30-100 (Rapid Learning): The model achieves high fidelity remarkably fast. By Epoch 30, the Generator has already mastered the stroke weights of digits like '0', '3', and '6'. By Epoch 100, the digits are structurally sound and exhibit the varied handwriting styles present in the training set.
  • Epoch 400 (Stable Equilibrium): The final state shows D_loss: 0.3255 and G_loss: 2.2475. This indicates that while the Generator is producing high-quality digits (2, 3, 8), the Discriminator still maintains a competitive edge, preventing the Generator from becoming "lazy" or undergoing mode collapse. The images are sharp, centered, and representative of the global MNIST distribution.
